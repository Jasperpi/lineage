## 1. 23andMe: the 2023 breach, settlements, UK fine, Chapter 11, the auction, the sale, deletions, AG actions and 2026 status

### Takeaway
A credential-stuffing attack on about 14,000 accounts (Apr–Sep 2023) exposed data on about 6.9 million people through the DNA Relatives sharing network. That set off fines, a class settlement of about $47M, a 2025 Chapter 11, and a $305M sale (closed 14 July 2025) to TTAM Research Institute, the nonprofit of co-founder Anne Wojcicki. The sale went ahead despite a suit by 28 attorneys general. About 1.9–2 million customers deleted their data. By mid-2026, 42 AGs had settled for $18M actually paid (on a $150M nominal claim), California had sued the shell company, and 23andMe had relaunched as the "23andMe Research Institute", holding DNA data on about 13 million people and aiming for 100 million users.

### Cited Findings

**The breach (historical, 2023)**
- The attacker ran a credential-stuffing attack between April and September 2023, reusing logins stolen in unrelated breaches. — [ICO, 17 Jun 2025](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/06/23andme-fined-for-failing-to-protect-uk-users-genetic-data/)
- About 14,000 accounts (about 0.1% of users) were directly compromised. Through them the attacker reached data on about 6.9M people: about 5.5M DNA Relatives profiles plus about 1.4M DNA Relatives users whose Family Tree profile information was accessed. — [TechCrunch, 4 Dec 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/12/04/23andme-confirms-hackers-stole-ancestry-data-on-6-9-million-users/) (search summary; figures consistent across [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/6-9-million-23andme-users-affected-by-data-breach/) and [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hackers-accessed-23andme-dna-data-millions-users/))
- 23andMe confirmed the breach on 6 Oct 2023. California alleges the attacker went undetected for five months and that data on about 1M consumers was offered for sale on the dark web, advertised as belonging to Asian American and Pacific Islander and Jewish users. — [California AG press release, 28 May 2026](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-sues-chrome-holding-co-formerly-known-23andme-over-2023)
- Data exposed for UK users included names, birth years, city- or postcode-level location, profile images, race, ethnicity, family trees and health reports. — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/06/23andme-fined-for-failing-to-protect-uk-users-genetic-data/)
- The security failures the AGs cite: no password blocklist and no multi-factor authentication, no rate limiting or intrusion prevention, no logging or monitoring to detect a breach, spikes in unusual logins not investigated, and known vulnerabilities not fixed. — [Troutman Pepper privacy blog, Jul 2026](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2026/07/state-ags-reach-novel-multistate-settlement-with-genetic-testing-company-23andme/); [Maryland AG, 14 Jul 2026](https://oag.maryland.gov/News/pages/Attorney-General-Brown-Announces-Multistate-Settlement-of----Bankruptcy-Claims-Against-23andMe-Over-Genetic-Data-Breach-.aspx)
- Maryland AG: "23andMe learned about the breach months after impacted personal information became publicly available." — [Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/News/pages/Attorney-General-Brown-Announces-Multistate-Settlement-of----Bankruptcy-Claims-Against-23andMe-Over-Genetic-Data-Breach-.aspx)

**Fines and settlements**
- **UK ICO:** fined 23andMe £2.31M on 17 June 2025 over a breach affecting 155,592 UK residents. The failings: no mandatory MFA, weak password rules, no unpredictable usernames, weak controls on access to raw genetic data, and poor threat monitoring. The investigation was joint with Canada's Office of the Privacy Commissioner. — [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/06/23andme-fined-for-failing-to-protect-uk-users-genetic-data/)
- The ICO had provisionally planned a £4.59M fine. — [Computing, Jun 2025](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2025/legislation-regulation/ico-fines-23andme-2-31m) (search summary)
- **US consumer class action:** settled for $30M in September 2024 (historical). — [Troutman](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2026/07/state-ags-reach-novel-multistate-settlement-with-genetic-testing-company-23andme/)
- Inside the bankruptcy the deal was redone as a settlement of up to $50M. The court gave preliminary approval on 5 Sep 2025. It covers about 6.4M US residents who were customers between 1 May and 1 Oct 2023, and offered cash up to $10,000 plus five years of genetic monitoring. — [HIPAA Journal](https://www.hipaajournal.com/23andme-class-action-data-breach-settlement/); [ClaimDepot](https://www.claimdepot.com/settlements/23-and-me-data-settlement) (search summary)
- A June 2026 filing by the plan administrator set the distribution at $46.75M: about $32.5M for class claims and more than $14M to Kroll, the claims administrator. — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/23andme-data-breach-claimants-to-receive-47-million-under-deal); [OpenClassActions](https://openclassactions.com/settlements/23-and-me-data-breach-class-action-settlement-2026.php) (search summary). The Vermont AG independently confirms the $46.75M figure. — [Vermont AG, 14 Jul 2026](https://ago.vermont.gov/blog/2026/07/14/attorney-general-clark-joins-multistate-genetic-data-breach-settlement-23andme-bankruptcy)
- The Chapter 11 plan provides up to $62M in all for breach claims: $30–50M for the US class, $3.25M for a Canadian class and $9M for arbitration claimants. — [Paul, Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-news/23andme-obtains-court-approval-of-chapter-11-plan)
- **Multistate AG settlement, 14 July 2026:** 42 AGs joined (Maryland counts 42 states plus DC). The nominal figure is $150M, but only $18M is actually paid from the bankruptcy estate and split among the states; Vermont receives $154,000. Troutman calls it "the largest AG multistate settlement involving a breach of consumer genetic data" and an "unprecedented AG intervention in bankruptcy proceedings". — [Troutman](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2026/07/state-ags-reach-novel-multistate-settlement-with-genetic-testing-company-23andme/); [Vermont AG](https://ago.vermont.gov/blog/2026/07/14/attorney-general-clark-joins-multistate-genetic-data-breach-settlement-23andme-bankruptcy); [Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/News/pages/Attorney-General-Brown-Announces-Multistate-Settlement-of----Bankruptcy-Claims-Against-23andMe-Over-Genetic-Data-Breach-.aspx)
- The terms binding TTAM (now 23andMe Research Institute): stronger data-security requirements, risk analysis, an Advisory Board, "an agreement to be bound by comprehensive privacy laws without exception", and continued consumer deletion rights. — [Vermont AG](https://ago.vermont.gov/blog/2026/07/14/attorney-general-clark-joins-multistate-genetic-data-breach-settlement-23andme-bankruptcy); [Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/News/pages/Attorney-General-Brown-Announces-Multistate-Settlement-of----Bankruptcy-Claims-Against-23andMe-Over-Genetic-Data-Breach-.aspx)
- Residents affected: 94,298 in Maryland ([Maryland AG](https://oag.maryland.gov/News/pages/Attorney-General-Brown-Announces-Multistate-Settlement-of----Bankruptcy-Claims-Against-23andMe-Over-Genetic-Data-Breach-.aspx)); about 14,000 in Vermont ([Vermont AG](https://ago.vermont.gov/blog/2026/07/14/attorney-general-clark-joins-multistate-genetic-data-breach-settlement-23andme-bankruptcy)); 855,541 in California ([CA AG](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-sues-chrome-holding-co-formerly-known-23andme-over-2023)).
- **California's separate suit:** AG Rob Bonta sued Chrome Holding Co. ("formerly known as 23andMe") in San Francisco Superior Court, announced 28 May 2026. The claims are under California's Genetic Information Privacy Act, its reasonable data-security law, the False Advertising Law, the Unfair Competition Law and the CCPA. The suit is "separate from the Attorney General's pending challenge in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Eastern District of Missouri regarding the sale of Californians' genetic information." — [CA AG](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-sues-chrome-holding-co-formerly-known-23andme-over-2023); coverage: [The Register, 29 May 2026](https://www.theregister.com/legal/2026/05/29/rob-bonta-sues-23andmes-new-owners-over-2023-breach/5248565)

### Inferences
- The size of the breach came from the relative-matching network, not from the raw files alone: about 14,000 stolen logins exposed about 6.9M people. A tool with no accounts, no cross-user matching and no server-side store has no equivalent attack surface. This is the sharpest contrast Lineage can draw.
- The bankruptcy showed that consent given to a company can pass to a new owner with the company's assets. The AGs and the FTC could shape the terms of the sale but did not stop it. What customers could actually do was delete their data (about 15% did). Any service that holds DNA carries this "sold with the company" risk; software that never receives the file does not.
- The 2026 settlement terms have become the expected trust vocabulary for incumbents: binding privacy law "without exception", an advisory board, reporting to AGs, deletion rights, ISO audits and 2-step verification. Lineage's equivalent has to be verifiable architecture, not promises (see section 6).
- 23andMe is again pursuing scale (100M users, AI). The large centralised databases are not shrinking, so the privacy-versus-network-effects trade-off stays live for consumers.

- No source found saying whether the ICO fine was actually paid out of the bankruptcy estate.
- Who sits on the 23andMe Research Institute advisory or privacy board, and its 2026 prices, were not found.
- No cumulative deletion total after June 2025 beyond "about 2 million".
- The CNBC web-traffic figures are unverified.

## 2. Ancestry, MyHeritage, FamilyTreeDNA and GEDmatch: ownership, database sizes, law-enforcement policies and incidents, 2023–2026

### Takeaway
The market has split in two. Ancestry, MyHeritage and 23andMe refuse law-enforcement use; Ancestry tightened its terms in August 2025 and MyHeritage stopped accepting raw-file uploads in 2025. FamilyTreeDNA (about 75% of profiles opted in) and GEDmatch (owned by the forensic company Qiagen, opt-in by default) are built for law-enforcement matching. With MyHeritage uploads gone and openSNP closed (see section 5), GEDmatch is the main place left to upload a raw file, and it is designed around police matching. No breach at these four companies in 2023–2026 was found.

### Cited Findings

**Ancestry**
- Blackstone bought Ancestry in December 2020 for $4.7B in an all-stock deal (historical). On 1 May 2023 the Seventh Circuit held that "a run-of-the-mill corporate acquisition" is not a compelled disclosure under Illinois GIPA (*Bridges v. Blackstone*). — [Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/seventh-circuit-says-all-stock-acquisition-without-more-does-not-trigger-liability-under-illinois-genetic-information-privacy-act); [Justia opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca7/22-2486/22-2486-2023-05-01.html)
- **DNA network size (unverified, conflicting):** "over 27 million" in Ancestry's own marketing as surfaced by search ([Ancestry RootsTech exhibitor page](https://www.familysearch.org/mn/rootstech/expohall/ancestry), search summary), versus "more than 30 million as of May 2026" from a third-party review site ([KnowYourDNA](https://knowyourdna.com/ancestry-kit-review/), search summary).
- **Law-enforcement stance:** Ancestry requires legal process, and for DNA "at a minimum … a court order or search warrant." In 2025 it received 0 valid requests for DNA data. It received 9 valid requests for non-DNA data (fraud, identity theft) and produced data for 7. It had no requests from outside the US and no classified requests as of 31 Dec 2025. — [Ancestry Transparency Report](https://www.ancestry.com/c/transparency/govt-and-law-enforcement) (search summary); [Ancestry law-enforcement guide](https://www.ancestry.com/c/legal/lawenforcement)
- **Terms update, 18 Aug 2025:** users agree "Not to use the Services in connection with any law enforcement investigation or judicial proceeding". AncestryDNA data may not be used for medical or diagnostic purposes, paternity or parentage, or law enforcement, including by uploading it elsewhere to get around the rule. — [Genealogy Bargains](https://genealogybargains.com/ancestry-terms-and-conditions-update/)

**MyHeritage**
- Owned by Francisco Partners since 2021. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MyHeritage) (not fetched; secondary)
- More than 9.3 million DNA users as of May 2025. — [The DNA Geek, 29 May 2025](https://thednageek.com/the-end-of-an-era-uploads-at-myheritage/)
- Its terms prohibit using the DNA services for "law enforcement purposes, forensic examinations, criminal investigations … without a court order and without prior explicit written permission from MyHeritage". — [MyHeritage policy as quoted by search](https://www.myheritage.com/privacy-policy) (search summary)
- **Raw-file uploads ended.** MyHeritage began blocking uploads about a week before 29 May 2025, with the message "no longer supported". MyHeritage gave no public reason; the blogger links it to law-enforcement use of MyHeritage coming to light in the Idaho (Kohberger) case after a *Dateline* broadcast on 9 May 2025. — [The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/the-end-of-an-era-uploads-at-myheritage/). MyHeritage's help centre now says uploads are not supported and tells users to buy a MyHeritage kit. — [MyHeritage Help](https://www.myheritage.com/help/en/articles/12852724-why-can-t-i-upload-my-dna-data-file) (search summary). One third-party site dates the change to March 2026 ([terms.law](https://terms.law/Privacy-Watchdog/dna-testing/myheritage/)), which conflicts with the May 2025 contemporaneous report.
- **Kohberger case:** defence filings reported in January 2025 argued that the FBI searched MyHeritage and another database in breach of their terms and the DOJ interim policy. Prosecutors argued that breaking a site's terms is not a constitutional violation. — [Spokesman-Review, 24 Jan 2025](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2025/jan/24/kohbergers-idaho-attorneys-argue-fbi-skirted-polic/)

**FamilyTreeDNA (FTDNA)**
- FTDNA is run by Gene by Gene, which myDNA acquired in 2021; the ultimate owner is an Australian businessman. — [MIT Technology Review, 22 Aug 2025](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/08/22/1122315/i-gave-police-access-to-my-dna/)
- Of about 1.5 million profiles, "three-quarters … were 'opted in'" to law-enforcement matching, according to general manager Dave Vance. The policy has changed back and forth; at one point every user was included by default. Police see only matches and "cannot search or 'dig through'" profiles. — [MIT Technology Review, 22 Aug 2025](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/08/22/1122315/i-gave-police-access-to-my-dna/)
- In 2019 the DNA Doe Project described FTDNA's database as "about a million kits" (historical). — [DNA Doe Project](https://dnadoeproject.org/message-about-the-recent-changes-at-gedmatch/)

**GEDmatch**
- Qiagen acquired Verogen, GEDmatch's owner, in 2023. In 2024 Qiagen partnered with Bode Technology to expand GEDmatch PRO, the law-enforcement portal; the consumer site is run separately by a Qiagen subsidiary. — [Qiagen press release, 2024](https://corporate.qiagen.com/English/newsroom/press-releases/press-release-details/2024/QIAGEN-and-Bode-Technology-partner-to-advance-use-of-GEDmatch-PRO-forensic-genetic-genealogy-database/default.aspx)
- More than 1.5 million profiles as of 2025. — [GEDmatch education page](https://www.gedmatch.com/education/what-is-gedmatch/) (search summary)
- **Privacy settings:**
  - Public Opt-in: your kit "WILL be compared with kits submitted by law enforcement to identify perpetrators of violent crimes".
  - Public Opt-out.
  - Personal Research: hidden from other users' match lists, including law-enforcement kits.
  - Private: no matching at all.
  - Police may also match to identify human remains. GEDmatch says uploaded raw files are "deleted" after processing and the data is "encoded".
  - [GEDmatch Privacy & Security](https://www.gedmatch.com/privacy-security/)
- Since 18 May 2019, law-enforcement matching has required users to opt in (historical). — [DNA Doe Project](https://dnadoeproject.org/message-about-the-recent-changes-at-gedmatch/)
- In a July 2020 breach, users' profiles were exposed to law-enforcement matching without their choice (historical). — [TechCrunch, 22 Jul 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/07/22/gedmatch-investigating-dna-profile-law-enforcement)

### Inferences
- Putting a raw file anywhere now means making a law-enforcement decision: opt-in by default at GEDmatch, a mostly opted-in database at FTDNA, and prohibited but evidently not prevented at MyHeritage (Kohberger). A tool that analyses locally and holds no match database makes no such decision for the user. Lineage should still say plainly that exporting to GEDmatch or FTDNA brings that exposure back.
- Upload-based analysis is shrinking: MyHeritage stopped uploads in 2025, openSNP closed in April 2025, and GEDmatch is owned by a forensic vendor. That leaves room for analysis without upload.
- Ancestry's August 2025 terms bar using its data "in connection with" law enforcement and bar uploading it elsewhere to get around that. People re-analysing AncestryDNA files in third-party tools may need a plain statement of how Lineage fits (personal, non-forensic use). This needs a legal read of the terms.

### Gaps
- No primary, dated 2026 figures for the size of the Ancestry or MyHeritage databases.
- GEDmatch's current opt-in rate, and FTDNA's current default for new US users, were not found.
- MyHeritage's official reason for ending uploads, and whether existing uploaded kits were kept, were not found.
- No 2023–2026 security incidents at Ancestry, MyHeritage, FTDNA or GEDmatch were found. This means none were found, not that none happened.

## 3. Investigative genetic genealogy (IGG/FIGG): policies, laws, 2024–2026 developments, opt-in rates

### Takeaway
The only national rule is still the DOJ's 2019 *interim* policy. It covers federal and DOJ-funded cases only: violent crimes and unidentified remains, CODIS checked first, only sites that tell users police may search, and no arrest on a genetic match alone. Only a few states regulate IGG: Maryland and Montana (2021) and Utah (2023). The Kohberger case (guilty plea 2 July 2025; in July 2026 he moved to withdraw it) showed the FBI searching MyHeritage despite its terms, and courts are accepting newer forensic sequencing (the Gilgo Beach ruling, 3 Sep 2025). The one opt-in rate found is FTDNA's, about 75% (August 2025).

### Cited Findings
- **DOJ interim policy.** Approved 2 Sep 2019 and effective 1 Nov 2019 (historical); announced 24 Sep 2019. ### Inferences
- The DOJ policy works through genealogy services, not through software: it binds investigators to services that notify users that police may search. A local analysis tool with no user-to-user matching is not a "GG service" in that sense, and holds nothing that could be searched or subpoenaed. This is an inference and needs legal review before being used as a public claim.
- Kohberger showed that a ban in a company's terms of service does not stop federal investigators. For privacy-minded users, "the company forbids it" is weaker than "no database exists".
- Public opinion is split, not hostile: 61% back at least violent-crime use. Lineage's messaging should be neutral: it is the user's choice, the tool does not make it for them.

## 4. Laws: US state genetic privacy laws 2023–2026, Illinois GIPA, federal bills, BIOSECURE, the DOJ bulk-data rule, EU and UK

### Takeaway
Since the 23andMe collapse, states have legislated quickly. In 2025: Montana, Texas, Indiana and Florida. In 2026: South Dakota, Utah, Connecticut, Vermont and Rhode Island. The model is separate express consent for each use, deletion and sample destruction, security duties, and (Texas, Utah, Florida) controls on foreign adversaries and on transfers in bankruptcy. Federally, only national-security measures became law: the DOJ bulk-data rule (in force 8 Apr 2025) and the BIOSECURE Act (18 Dec 2025). The two consumer-protection bills, the Genomic Data Protection Act and the Don't Sell My DNA Act, were introduced but not found to be enacted. Under the EU and UK GDPR, genetic data is special-category data that can generally be processed only with explicit consent.

### Inferences
- Nearly every new duty falls on whoever collects, stores, transfers or sells genetic data: consent per use, deletion, sample destruction, security programmes, bans on foreign adversaries and on transfers in bankruptcy, annual certifications. A tool that never receives the file keeps its compliance surface small and can say why. Whether browser-only analysis software falls within a "direct-to-consumer genetic testing company" (some statutes cover companies that analyse data consumers provide) needs statute-by-statute legal review; that was not verified here.
- Under the GDPR, if no personal data reaches the operator, the operator is plausibly not processing genetic data at all. Hosting logs such as IP addresses, telemetry, and third-party fonts or CDNs are still personal data. So "no analytics, no third-party requests" matters legally as well as for trust. This is an inference and needs legal review.
- The FTC's 1Health case shows what regulators punish in small DNA firms: deletion claims that are not true and retroactive changes to privacy terms. A local-first tool's privacy claims have to be literally true and stay true across versions, or they risk being deceptive under Section 5 of the FTC Act.

## 5. Market size and trend, 2023–2026, and consumer attitudes after the 23andMe bankruptcy

  - About 1.9M deletion requests by June 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/11/23andme-says-15-of-customers-asked-to-delete-their-genetic-data-since-bankruptcy/)), and about 2M by 2026 ([Bloomberg via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/healthcare/articles/23andme-targets-100-million-users-201316216.html)).
  - openSNP closed on 30 Apr 2025 and deleted its data, after about 14 years and with about 13,000 users. Co-founder Bastian Greshake Tzovaras cited 23andMe's collapse and "the rise in authoritarian governments", and noted that most openSNP data came from 23andMe files. — [TechCrunch, 1 Apr 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/01/genetic-sharing-site-opensnp-to-shut-down-citing-concerns-of-data-privacy-and-rise-in-authoritarian-governments/); [Tzovaras retrospective](https://tzovar.as/sunsetting-opensnp/)
- **Academic literature from 2025 (not fetched):**
  - "Public concerns about direct-to-consumer DNA test kits: the evidence from survey and social media data", *New Genetics and Society* (2025). — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14636778.2025.2456259)
  - Raeiro et al., a "privacy calculus" study of DTC genetic testing, *Journal of Consumer Affairs* (2025). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/joca.70008)

### Inferences
- The optimistic market reports and the audited 23andMe figures disagree. The likely reading is that one-time ancestry kits are saturated and shrinking, while money has moved to subscriptions, health and clinical testing, and research. The people Lineage serves are the tens of millions who already hold a raw file (21% of US adults have tested, per YouGov) and want more from it without handing it to another company.
- Privacy has become the main objection. A privacy-first tool therefore addresses the leading stated barrier, not a niche worry. The 2 million deletions show many people will act on that concern.
- openSNP's closure is a precedent Lineage can use: even an open-science upload site decided holding DNA had become a liability. It also removed a public place to put 23andMe-era files.

### Gaps
- No nationally representative survey taken after March 2025 (after the bankruptcy) was found. The YouGov survey predates it by about ten weeks.
- No reliable 2023–2026 kit-volume data for Ancestry, MyHeritage or FTDNA, which are private companies.
- The market-report pages could not be fetched; their methods and exact dates are unknown.

## 6. What would make a local-first, open-source DNA analysis tool credible (audits, reproducibility, no accounts, offline use, clear licences)

### Takeaway
The 2023–2026 record teaches three things. Promises about data fail when a company is breached, sold or closes (23andMe, Atlas Biomed, openSNP). Regulators punish privacy claims that turn out to be false (FTC v. 1Health). Incumbents now sell trust with audits and certifications (23andMe's ISO audits). A local-first tool becomes credible when users and third parties can check its privacy claims instead of trusting them. The established ideas: local-first principles (offline use, longevity, user ownership), reproducible builds, published hashes of the code a site serves (as in Meta's Code Verify), independent audits, no accounts or telemetry, and clear licences.

### Cited Findings
- **Local-first ideals** (Kleppmann, Wiggins, van Hardenberg, McGranaghan, *Onward!* 2019): no waiting on a server; works on multiple devices; the network is optional; collaboration; "The Long Now" (data stays readable after the software's makers are gone); security and privacy by default ("Your local devices store only your own data, avoiding the centralized cloud database holding everybody's data"); and the user's ultimate ownership and control. — [Ink & Switch](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/)
- **Reproducible builds:** "A build is reproducible if given the same source code, build environment and build instructions, any party can recreate bit-by-bit identical copies of all specified artifacts." Anyone can then check, by comparing hashes, that the shipped code matches the source. — [reproducible-builds.org](https://reproducible-builds.org/docs/definition/)
- **Checking web-delivered code:** Meta's open-source Code Verify extension (10 Mar 2022) compares the hash of the JavaScript served by WhatsApp Web against a hash Cloudflare publishes independently, and flags any mismatch. A web app can change the code it serves on any request, so a web tool's privacy claims need a way to verify the code. — [Meta Engineering](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/03/10/security/code-verify/); [The Register, 10 Mar 2022](https://www.theregister.com/2022/03/10/whatsapp_cloudflare_code/) (search summary)
- **The incumbents' trust signals in 2026:** 23andMe advertises ISO/IEC 27001, 27701 and 27018 systems audited annually, 2-step verification, research consent that can be changed, a stance of never voluntarily releasing data to law enforcement, insurers or public databases, and a transparency report twice a year. — [23andMe Trust Center, updated 30 Apr 2026](https://www.23andme.org/trust-center/). Ancestry publishes a transparency report on law-enforcement requests. — [Ancestry](https://www.ancestry.com/c/transparency)
- **What regulators punish in small DNA firms:** false claims about deletion, retroactive changes to privacy policies, and poor security (1Health.io, 2023). — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/ftc-says-genetic-testing-company-1health-failed-protect-privacy-security-dna-data-unfairly-changed). Deceptive claims about accuracy (CRI Genetics, 2023). — [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/12/dna-testing-provider-settles-ftc-and-ca-ag-claims)
- **When a company disappears:** Atlas Biomed, a London DNA firm with links to Russia, went dark (reported November 2024). Its offices were closed and customers were locked out of results that could only be viewed online and could not be downloaded. It had filed no accounts since December 2022, and the ICO received a complaint. — [Malwarebytes, Nov 2024](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2024/11/dna-testing-company-vanishes-along-with-its-customers-genetic-data); [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/security/dna-testing-firm-vanishes-with-highly-sensitive-data/) (search summary)
- **An open, non-profit upload site closed** in April 2025 over the risks of centralised genetic data (openSNP). — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/01/genetic-sharing-site-opensnp-to-shut-down-citing-concerns-of-data-privacy-and-rise-in-authoritarian-governments/)
- **Users' stated barriers** are privacy (42%) and distrust of companies (36%). — [YouGov, Feb 2025](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/51527-what-americans-think-about-home-dna-tests-poll)

### Inferences
A checklist drawn from the findings above; each point is an inference, not a sourced standard.
1. **Make "the file never leaves the device" checkable.**
   - Ship a strict Content Security Policy (`connect-src 'none'` or `'self'`, with no third-party script, font or analytics origins) and publish it.
   - Say how to confirm it: the browser's network tab shows zero requests after the file is loaded; better, the tool works with the network turned off.
   - This answers the 1Health lesson that claims must be literally true.
2. **Offline and long-lived** ("The Long Now"). Make the app work offline as a PWA or as a downloadable single-file or static release that runs from disk. Reports should be downloadable and in open formats, so a user can still read them if lineage.jaspermeller.com is gone. This is the opposite of Atlas Biomed's online-only results.
3. **Reproducible, signed releases.**
   - Build deterministically, tag releases, and publish SHA-256 hashes of the deployed bundle.
   - Optionally add a Code-Verify-style page or a documented `curl | sha256sum` check that the served JavaScript matches the tagged source.
   - Pin and hash the reference data (SNP annotations, allele frequencies) so the same input file and version always give the same output; this is analytical reproducibility.
4. **No accounts, no telemetry, no server store.** With nothing to breach, sell in bankruptcy, subpoena or match against, the 23andMe breach, sale and law-enforcement scenarios cannot happen. The tool should say this structurally ("there is no database") rather than as a policy ("we promise not to…").
5. **Independent review.** Commission and publish a short third-party audit of the data-flow claim and the dependency tree; it can be scoped small because the attack surface is small. That matches the audit language incumbents use (23andMe's annual ISO audits) at an open-source tool's scale.
6. **Clear licences for code and data.** Use an OSI licence for the code. For the knowledge base, state the licence of each source; some SNP annotation sources carry non-commercial or share-alike terms (verify each). State the terms of use for each vendor's file format, including Ancestry's August 2025 restrictions, and make clear the tool is not for medical diagnosis or forensic use.
7. **Honest scope and accuracy.** State the limits of consumer genotyping arrays and imputation, and cite the sources for each interpretation. Avoid health claims beyond the evidence (the CRI Genetics lesson).
8. **Name the exits.** Show plainly what happens if a user exports to GEDmatch or FTDNA (opt-in or default law-enforcement matching), so the privacy guarantee is not over-read.
9. **Positioning versus small companies and upload sites.** Their weak points are documented: centralised storage (23andMe breach), transfer in bankruptcy (the TTAM sale), disappearing (Atlas Biomed), closing (openSNP), dropping uploads (MyHeritage), and law-enforcement matching (GEDmatch, FTDNA). A local-first tool avoids each of them by construction.

### Gaps
- No survey data was found on how consumers judge "local-first" or "client-side" privacy claims, or which proofs (audits, open source, offline mode) they find persuasive.
- No precedent was found of an independent security audit of a browser-based consumer-DNA analysis tool.
- Existing local or offline DNA tools and their licences were not surveyed; the parallel research threads may cover them.
- The licence terms of specific SNP annotation databases (e.g., SNPedia and ClinVar) were not checked in this pass.
- No legal opinion was found on whether client-side-only analysis falls under state direct-to-consumer genetic-testing statutes or makes the operator a GDPR controller of genetic data. This needs counsel.
