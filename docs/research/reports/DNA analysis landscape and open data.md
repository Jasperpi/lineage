# Hold No DNA, Show Every Source

Lineage cannot beat the small DNA-upload and analysis companies by matching strangers, because a browser tool cannot do that. It can beat them by giving away the analyses they now charge $15 a month to $844 a tier for, without the file ever leaving the device, and by putting an open dataset, a published method, a sample size and an interval behind every number it shows. The market has moved Lineage's way. Between 2023 and 2026 MyHeritage, Geneanet and openSNP stopped taking raw files. FamilyTreeDNA pushed uploaders toward retesting. GEDmatch, owned by the forensic firm Qiagen, raised its Tier 1 price by half for new subscribers. Privacy became the most common reason Americans give for not testing, cited by 42%. The ancient-ancestry sellers mostly repackage published genomes. The Allen Ancient DNA Resource alone now offers about 17,600 ancient individuals under CC0, almost five times the 3,578 ancient samples in the engine behind MyHeritage's Ancient Origins. The literature supports five kinds of result: relationship likelihoods from segment counts and lengths, similarity to the 26 1000 Genomes and 54 HGDP reference populations, runs-of-homozygosity totals read against published landmarks, haplogroups shown beside dated ancient carriers, and eye-colour probabilities from HIrisPlex-S. It does not support a personal "43% Yamnaya", chromosome painting finer than continents, ancient "matches" from chip files, or any face generated from DNA. The interface should lead with intervals, reference-sample points and the segments behind each prediction, not bare percentages on national maps. Two changes should come before any new feature. First, bundle the reference panel so the web app makes no network request after a file is loaded. Second, drop the roadmap's public research database, which would recreate the custody risk that is Lineage's clearest advantage.

## Three server-side networks are all that remain for uploads

Between 2023 and 2026 the number of places that accept another company's raw file fell to three. Geneanet removed its DNA features and deleted every raw file on 20 December 2023 ([Geneanet](https://en.geneanet.org/genealogyblog/post/2023/11/geneanet-dna-features-will-be-discontinued)). openSNP shut down and deleted its data on 30 April 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/01/genetic-sharing-site-opensnp-to-shut-down-citing-concerns-of-data-privacy-and-rise-in-authoritarian-governments/)). **MyHeritage, with 9.6 million kits, stopped accepting uploads in late May 2025** and now tells uploaders to buy its kit instead ([MyHeritage help](https://www.myheritage.com/help/en/articles/12852724-why-can-t-i-upload-my-dna-data-file); [The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/database-sizes-march-2026/)). AncestryDNA (about 30 million kits) and 23andMe (just under 14 million) have never taken uploads ([DNAeXplained](https://dna-explained.com/2025/10/14/myheritage-introduces-a-low-pass-whole-genome-autosomal-dna-test-why-it-matters/)).

That leaves GEDmatch, FamilyTreeDNA and Living DNA, and all three are tightening. **GEDmatch is operated by Verogen, a Qiagen subsidiary. On 1 September 2026 it raised Tier 1 for new subscribers from $10 to $15 a month, or from $100 to $150 a year,** and it will retire its current site in March 2027 ([GEDmatch terms](https://www.gedmatch.com/terms-of-service-november-22-2024/); [GEDmatch blog](https://www.gedmatch.com/blog/see-whats-coming-the-new-gedmatch-interface-preview-is-now-on-youtube/)). **FamilyTreeDNA retired its $19 unlock for transferred kits on 18 August 2026.** Matching stays free, but advanced tools now require a new NGS test ($29 promotional price), because upcoming features "cannot be supported on files from other vendors" ([DNAeXplained](https://dna-explained.com/2026/08/13/autosomal-dna-unlock-at-familytreedna-to-be-retired-august-18th/)). Living DNA still offers a free upload with eight continental regions, and its help centre describes the matching as in beta ([Living DNA](https://livingdna.com/free-dna-upload); [Living DNA support](https://support.livingdna.com/hc/en-us/articles/360026178111-What-will-I-receive-if-I-upload-my-raw-data)).

The larger change is in the analysis layer above the matching, where features users once had are being sold back to them. Ancestry added match clustering on 10 July 2025 inside Pro Tools, reported at $10 a month on top of a membership ([Ancestry help](https://help.ancestry.com/hc/en-us/articles/53933348842131); [Ancestry offer page](https://www.ancestry.com/offers/join?feature=proTools); [DNAeXplained](https://dna-explained.com/2025/12/31/2025-genetic-genealogy-retrospective-wow-what-a-year/)). 23andMe switched off segment data after its 2023 breach. On 27 February 2026 it restored a chromosome browser only for 23andMe+ subscribers tested on its V5 chip, with no downloads. Because deceased relatives cannot retest, one genealogist called the change "desperate, tone deaf, and extortionist" ([Through the Trees](https://throughthetreesblog.tumblr.com/post/810909158988709888/23andme-an-unfortunate-update)). MyHeritage keeps AutoClusters and Theory of Family Relativity behind a site subscription ([MyHeritage help](https://www.myheritage.com/help/en/articles/12852419-what-are-the-advanced-myheritage-dna-features)). The independent tools charge too: DNA Painter costs $55 a year ([DNA Painter](https://dnapainter.com/help/how-much)), and Genetic Affairs sells server-time credits at $5–50 a month ([Genetic Affairs](https://geneticaffairs.com/prices.html)).

Segment data is scarce. As of February 2026 only FamilyTreeDNA and GEDmatch export it in bulk. MyHeritage and Living DNA give it one match at a time, 23andMe gives subscribers restricted per-match views, and **Ancestry has never offered segment data at all** ([DNA Painter tracker](https://dnapainter.com/blog/what-is-going-on-with-segment-data/)). Every one of these services runs on its own servers. The only local alternatives found are installable desktop programs such as DNAGedcom and the Borland toolkit ([DNAGedcom](https://www.dnagedcom.com/); [ISOGG](https://isogg.org/wiki/Borland_Genetics_Desktop_Toolkit)).

Test formats are also moving away from the text files every third-party tool reads. **MyHeritage moved all new kits to low-pass whole-genome sequencing in October 2025 and supplies raw data as CRAM files.** **FamilyTreeDNA's Family Finder has run on a Twist Bioscience sequencing panel covering about 280 million base pairs since 2 March 2026** ([DNAeXplained](https://dna-explained.com/2025/10/14/myheritage-introduces-a-low-pass-whole-genome-autosomal-dna-test-why-it-matters/); [FTDNA press release](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/893145322/familytreedna-advances-genetic-genealogy-with-precision-genomics-partnership)). The exact download formats for both are unverified. Either way, a tool that reads only 23andMe-style genotype text will cover a shrinking share of new tests. Lineage today reads raw files from five named vendors plus generic layouts. Reading genotype VCF files is the practical next format. Decoding CRAM in a browser needs a reference genome and is a heavy engineering job, so documented conversion is the realistic first answer.

What users complain about most is results that move without explanation. After Ancestry's 2025 update, posters on Mumsnet reported going "27% Germanic Europe to 0%" and "from 33% Scottish to not at all" ([Mumsnet](https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/genealogy/5425817-ancestry-dna-update-2025)). MyHeritage went from 42 to 79 ethnicities in a single release ([MyHeritage](https://blog.myheritage.com/2025/02/introducing-ethnicity-estimate-v2-5-improved-dna-ethnicity-model/)). Ancestry's update, announced on 24 September 2026, doubles its regions again with "a brand new region assignment algorithm" ([Ancestry](https://www.ancestry.com/c/ancestry-blog/dna/2026-ancestral-origins-coming-soon)). Leah Larkin's 2021 complaint about GEDmatch, "There is no explanation of offset, limit, cM size, tag groups, or overlap cutoff", describes the same opacity at the level of individual settings ([The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/gedmatch-has-a-makeover-but-its-purely-cosmetic/)).

The strategic reading is that Lineage cannot win on the one thing these networks own, a database of strangers to match against, and should not try. It can win as the analysis layer for data people already hold: their own raw file, a relative's file, and the match and segment exports that FamilyTreeDNA, GEDmatch and MyHeritage still allow. That analysis should run on versioned reference data, with a documented setting behind every threshold. It must read only files that users export themselves. Ancestry's 2020 cease-and-desist to Genetic Affairs shows what scraping invites ([Dana Leeds](https://www.danaleeds.com/ancestry_genetic_affairs_cease/)).

## Ancient-ancestry sellers rent out public genomes by the tier

The "fun" end of the market takes a consumer chip file and places it in a space built from published ancient genomes. The result is sold as tiers, maps and named ancestors. **MyTrueAncestry has nine levels, from a free tier matched against 10 ancient samples to an $844 tier matched against 500 or more.** Features such as 2-D and 3-D PCA, archaeological maps and "Genetic Social Groups" unlock along the way ([MyTrueAncestry prices](https://mytrueancestry.com/en/prices)). Its site names no algorithm, SNP count or sample source, yet it claims to be "the only service powered by real ancient DNA samples" ([MyTrueAncestry](https://mytrueancestry.com/en/)).

Illustrative DNA, an Estonian company, says its DeepAncestry product projects raw data "onto a PCA-based data space" of 585 ancient populations (3,578 samples) and 632 modern ones ([Illustrative DNA](https://illustrativedna.com/deep-ancestry)). DeepAncestry powers MyHeritage's Ancient Origins, launched in February 2025 for Complete and Omni subscribers. MyHeritage passes Illustrative DNA only "an anonymous vector called DeepAncestry Coordinates", which has 25 dimensions ([MyHeritage blog](https://blog.myheritage.com/2025/02/introducing-ancient-origins-trace-your-origins-back-10000-years/)). In May 2026 MyHeritage added a "genetic proximity" score to 32 named historical individuals, including Ötzi and Beethoven, with AI-generated videos, while printing that the results "do not indicate direct descent" ([MyHeritage blog](https://blog.myheritage.com/2026/05/new-in-ancient-origins-discover-your-connection-to-notable-individuals-from-history/)).

The hobbyist Global25 system is a 25-component PCA built on about 300,000 SNPs. Its author concedes, "It's difficult to say how accurate these estimates are" ([Eurogenes](https://eurogenes.blogspot.com/2019/07/getting-most-out-of-global25_12.html)). A personal set of coordinates costs €15 from the official portal ([G25 Requests](https://g25requests.app/)). Because the projection space is private, only that portal can place a new sample in it ([Ancestrify, a competitor](https://www.ancestrify.io/blog/where-to-buy-g25-coordinates)). Ancestrify, founded in 2026, sells qpAdm ancestry models "with a p-value" for €29.99 ([Ancestrify](https://www.ancestrify.io/compare/illustrative-dna)).

What these sellers own is curation, labels, a private coordinate space and an interface. They do not own the genomes. **The Allen Ancient DNA Resource (AADR), version 66.p1, released on 8 June 2026 under CC0, holds about 17,600 ancient individuals genotyped at up to 2 million positions** ([Harvard Dataverse](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/FFIDCW)). A competitor reports that Illustrative DNA's own AdmixLab runs on AADR panels ([Ancestrify](https://www.ancestrify.io/compare/illustrative-dna)). MyTrueAncestry does not disclose where its samples come from, but its ladder is plainly a price on access to reference samples: what the report shows changes with the tier, not with the customer's DNA.

The one layer an open tool cannot reproduce is a private customer database. 23andMe's 275-plus "Historical Matches" rest on shared-segment comparisons against about 12.8 million customers, and the researchers who built them describe the limits on reproducing that work ([23andMe](https://www.23andme.org/blog/articles/from-research-to-real-insights-23andmes-new-product-features-for-2025); [Harney et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10502734/)).

The trait and health upload sector sells lookups from SNPs to traits, by the report or by subscription. Promethease charges $25 per report built on SNPedia ([Promethease](https://promethease.com/)). Xcode Life charges $30–69 per report or $139–199 per bundle ([Xcode Life](https://www.xcode.life/)). SelfDecode charges $319 to upload a file for its "1500+" reports ([SelfDecode](https://selfdecode.com/en/upload-dna-file/)). A 2026 review describes Sequencing.com's $5–99 apps as "not clinical" and costlier in total than their sticker price ([KnowYourDNA](https://knowyourdna.com/sequencing-review/)). In 2023 the FTC and California's attorney general reached an order with CRI Genetics over deceptive claims about the accuracy of its reports ([Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/enforcement-edge/2023/12/dna-testing-provider-settles-ftc-and-ca-ag-claims)).

Lineage admits only benign, well-replicated traits with a primary paper, and no disease, carrier or drug-response variants. That policy is on the right side of this line and should stay, which also means Lineage should not chase report counts. SNPedia's CC BY-NC-SA licence would in any case impose non-commercial, share-alike terms on anything built from it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SNPedia)).

What spreads on social media is identity storytelling: extinct peoples, named individuals, AI avatars, and quirky traits such as the "unibrow" and "sweet vs. salty preference" that 23andMe advertises ([23andMe](https://www.23andme.com/compare-dna-tests/)). What users distrust is process they cannot see. MyTrueAncestry scores 3.5 out of 5 on Trustpilot from 64 reviews, with complaints that "level tiering is getting ridiculous" and one report of weekly double charges ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/mytrueancestry.com)). MyHeritage warns that results "evolve over time" ([MyHeritage help](https://www.myheritage.com/help/en/articles/12870037-what-is-ancient-origins)). The 23andMe and Harvard authors of the ancient-DNA matching study ask companies to "not overstate the likelihood… of direct genealogical connections" and to make the caveats educational ([Harney et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10502734/)).

Lineage can meet the demand for a story honestly. A Y or mitochondrial branch shared with a dated, published ancient individual is a fact, not a projection. It can meet the distrust with what these sellers do not publish: sample lists, SNP overlap, fit statistics and a change log.

## Custody failures made privacy the leading reason not to test

The sector's defining failures in 2023–2026 were failures of custody, not of science. A credential-stuffing attack on about 14,000 23andMe accounts exposed data on about 6.9 million people, because the DNA Relatives network linked them ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/12/04/23andme-confirms-hackers-stole-ancestry-data-on-6-9-million-users/)). The UK's Information Commissioner fined the company £2.31 million ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2025/06/23andme-fined-for-failing-to-protect-uk-users-genetic-data/)). 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 on 23 March 2025. Its data passed to TTAM Research Institute for $305 million on 14 July 2025, despite a suit by 28 attorneys general ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/07/14/3114916/0/en/TTAM-Research-Institute-A-Nonprofit-Public-Benefit-Corporation-Completes-The-Acquisition-of-23andMe-Assets.html); [Pennsylvania AG](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/taking-action/attorney-general-sunday-joins-multi-state-lawsuit-to-block-23andmes-sale-of-consumer-data/)). **About 1.9 million customers, roughly 15%, asked for their data to be deleted** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/11/23andme-says-15-of-customers-asked-to-delete-their-genetic-data-since-bankruptcy/)). The settlement with 42 attorneys general on 14 July 2026 is nominally worth $150 million but pays out $18 million ([Troutman Pepper](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2026/07/state-ags-reach-novel-multistate-settlement-with-genetic-testing-company-23andme/)). The relaunched 23andMe Research Institute is aiming for 100 million users ([Bloomberg via Yahoo](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/healthcare/articles/23andme-targets-100-million-users-201316216.html)).

Two lessons follow. The size of the breach came from the relative-matching network, not from any single file. And consent given to a company travelled with its assets in bankruptcy: regulators shaped the sale but did not stop it.

Uploading has also become a decision about law enforcement. In July 2020 a breach at GEDmatch briefly opted all 1.45 million profiles into police matching ([BuzzFeed News](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/peteraldhous/hackers-gedmatch-dna-privacy)). From 2019 to 2023, practitioners could trick one of its tools into showing kits that had opted out ([The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2023/08/18/gedmatch-dna-police-forensic-genetic-genealogy/)). Security researchers showed that someone who only uploads normally formatted files can extract a target's genotype using 10–20 specially crafted kits ([NDSS 2020](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/genotype-extraction-and-false-relative-attacks-security-risks-to-third-party-genetic-genealogy-services-beyond-identity-inference/)). About three-quarters of FamilyTreeDNA profiles are opted in to police matching ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/08/22/1122315/i-gave-police-access-to-my-dna/)).

MyHeritage forbids police use, yet the FBI searched it in the Idaho murders investigation. Prosecutors argued, and the court accepted, that breaking a site's terms of service is not a constitutional violation ([Spokesman-Review](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2025/jan/24/kohbergers-idaho-attorneys-argue-fbi-skirted-polic/); [AP via AOL](https://www.aol.com/man-accused-idaho-college-student-205000822.html)). Ancestry's terms of 18 August 2025 bar using its data in connection with law enforcement, including by uploading it elsewhere ([Genealogy Bargains](https://genealogybargains.com/ancestry-terms-and-conditions-update/)). For a privacy-minded user, "no database exists" is a stronger guarantee than "the company forbids it". Lineage should still say plainly that exporting a file to GEDmatch or FamilyTreeDNA brings that exposure back.

Demand has shifted accordingly. In a YouGov survey in January 2025, 21% of American adults had taken a DNA test. **Privacy was the leading reason not to test, cited by 42%, ahead of distrust of companies at 36%**, and 30% called privacy "very much a concern", up from 25% in 2022 ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/51527-what-americans-think-about-home-dna-tests-poll)). 23andMe's customer count fell from about 15.1 million to 14.4 million in the year to March 2025 ([SEC 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1804591/000162828025030786/mehcq-20250331.htm)). Market-research estimates of direct-to-consumer testing revenue in 2025 run from $2.3 billion to $4.5 billion and cannot be reconciled ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/direct-to-consumer-genetic-testing-market-report); [GMI](https://www.gminsights.com/industry-analysis/direct-to-consumer-dtc-genetic-testing-market)).

The most likely reading is that kit sales are saturated. Lineage's audience is the people who have already tested and can download a raw file, and who want more from it without handing it to another company. No survey taken after the 23andMe bankruptcy was found, and no study of how consumers judge "local-first" privacy claims. The size of that audience is therefore inferred, not measured.

New law points the same way, because nearly every duty falls on whoever collects, stores or transfers genetic data. Montana, Texas, Indiana and Florida legislated in 2025, and South Dakota, Utah, Connecticut, Vermont and Rhode Island in 2026. The common elements are separate express consent for each use, deletion rights and security duties. Texas, Florida and Utah add rules aimed at foreign adversaries, and Texas bars transferring genomic data to them in bankruptcy ([Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/health-privacy/multiple-states-enact-genetic-privacy-legislation-in-a-busy-start-to-2025/); [Paubox](https://www.paubox.com/blog/utah-and-south-dakota-advance-genetic-privacy-rules-with-new-2026-laws); [Proskauer](https://privacylaw.proskauer.com/2026/06/articles/data-privacy-laws/from-data-brokers-to-dna-connecticut-enacts-sweeping-privacy-amendments/)). The Justice Department's bulk-data rule, in force since 8 April 2025, restricts transactions that give six countries of concern access to human genomic data on more than 100 US persons, even when that data is "anonymized, pseudonymized, de-identified, or encrypted" ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/nsd/data-security); [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2025/08/Navigating-Privacy-Gaps-and-New-Legal-Requirements-for-Companies-Processing-Genetic-Data)). The GDPR allows genetic data to be processed only under Article 9 exceptions, chiefly explicit consent ([GDPR Art. 9](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/)).

A tool that never receives a file keeps its exposure to these laws small. Whether browser-only analysis software falls under the state statutes, or makes its operator a GDPR controller, is unresolved and needs a lawyer's opinion. The FTC's 2023 order against 1Health.io sets the standard: it was punished for deletion claims that were untrue and for changing its privacy policy retroactively ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/06/ftc-says-genetic-testing-company-1health-failed-protect-privacy-security-dna-data-unfairly-changed)). Privacy claims must be literally true and stay true across versions.

Credibility for a local-first tool therefore rests on claims that users or auditors can check, not on promises. The established ingredients are three. The first is local-first design, where the network is optional and data stays readable after the software's makers are gone ([Ink & Switch](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/)). The second is reproducible builds, which let anyone confirm that the shipped code matches the source ([reproducible-builds.org](https://reproducible-builds.org/docs/definition/)). The third is independently published hashes of the JavaScript a site serves, which is what Meta's Code Verify checks for WhatsApp Web ([Meta Engineering](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/03/10/security/code-verify/)). When the London DNA firm Atlas Biomed went dark in 2024, its customers lost results that existed only online ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2024/11/dna-testing-company-vanishes-along-with-its-customers-genetic-data)). That is why Lineage's reports should download in open formats.

Measured against that standard, Lineage has two gaps today, both observed in the repository on 25 September 2026. First, the web app's Ancestry tab builds its reference panel by sending the rsIDs on the user's chip to Ensembl while the app runs. The page header discloses this ("sends only public rsID numbers to Ensembl"). The deployed Content-Security-Policy in `apps/web/vercel.json` sets only `frame-ancestors`, so nothing restricts where the page can connect. As a result, "nothing leaves this tab" cannot be tested by turning the network off.

Second, the README's roadmap proposes "a public, opt-in research database where people contribute anonymised results". openSNP's founder deleted a 14-year-old open database in 2025 because "the risk/benefit calculus of providing free & open access to individual genetic data in 2025 is very different" ([tzovar.as](https://tzovar.as/sunsetting-opensnp/)). DNA.Land deleted 163,000 genomes in 2019 rather than transfer them ([The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/big-changes-at-dna-land/)). A database would turn Lineage from a tool that holds nothing into a custodian exposed to every risk above. For the same reason, if the AI narration now in the command-line tool ever moves into the web app, it would send derived genetic results to a third party, so it must stay opt-in and clearly labelled.

## CC0 and citation-only data can outbuild the paid reference sets

The open reference base is larger than anything the small sellers show, and most of it can be redistributed as derived tables of counts and frequencies (aggregates). The assets below were verified and sized for this report.

| [openSNP 2017 dump](https://archive.org/details/opensnp_data_dumps) | About 21.4 GB of user genotypes | CC0 | Do not use: its founders chose to delete the service |

Only a few of these carry clear open licences: AADR (CC0), YTree (CC BY 4.0), the Haplogrep tree packaging (MIT), Natural Earth (public domain) and world-atlas (ISC). 1000 Genomes and HGDP are free of embargo and ask only for a citation, which is workable for derived aggregates ([IGSR disclaimer](https://www.internationalgenome.org/IGSR_disclaimer)).

The genetic maps are the weak point. None of the three usable GRCh37 maps carries a licence: Beagle's HapMap II map, Bhérer's sex-specific maps and the 9.75 GiB IMPUTE2 bundle ([Bhérer README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cbherer/Bherer_etal_SexualDimorphismRecombination/master/README.md); [IMPUTE2](https://mathgen.stats.ox.ac.uk/impute/1000GP_Phase3.html)). The README's roadmap item, "a permissively licensed genetic map", therefore describes something that does not exist in verified form. The defensible approach is the one the working tree already takes. An uncommitted, roughly 550 KB `data/reference/genetic-map.grch37.json` derives cM values from the Beagle map and records its source URL, SHA-256 and citation. The remaining step is to ask the map's authors for written permission.

Two more licence questions need settling before launch. The Shared cM Project v4 PDF contains no licence statement at all ([Shared cM Project v4](https://thegeneticgenealogist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Shared-cM-Project-Version-4.pdf)), yet Lineage's README and footer credit the table as CC BY 4.0 via DNA Painter. The project should keep a dated record of where that grant appears, or confirm it with Blaine Bettinger. The HIrisPlex-S regression coefficients sit in journal supplements and in Erasmus MC's web tool. The open tools examined either build an upload file for that web tool or tell users to submit to it ([aHISplex](https://github.com/zmaroti/aHISplex)). None found embeds the coefficients.

The AADR annotation file is the single most valuable asset. Stripped to ID, date, place, group, haplogroups and DOI, it shrinks from 13.4 MB to a few megabytes and gives an offline, citable map of published ancient individuals without redistributing a single genotype ([AADR annotation](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/api/access/datafile/13994515)). It needs careful handling. Groups carry site-and-period labels rather than textbook names: Western hunter-gatherers appear as `Serbia_IronGates_Mesolithic` or `Luxembourg_Loschbour_Mesolithic`, Anatolian farmers as `Turkey_N` (68 individuals), and 318 individuals sit under labels ending `_EBA_Yamnaya`. Any grouping into "hunter-gatherer", "farmer" or "steppe" must therefore be curated and published. Quality flags matter, because 18,549 rows are flagged "Pass", 962 "Questionable" and 373 "CRITICAL". The sampling is geographically lopsided, with 1,769 rows from Russia, 1,473 from Hungary and 1,385 from the UK, so a map should present density as where archaeologists sampled, not where anyone's ancestry lies. Versions must be pinned too. **Version 66.p1 exists because 161 modern individuals were released by mistake**, and the curators ask that decommissioned versions not be used ([AADR README](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/api/access/datafile/13994530)).

The modern references need the same care. IGSR's coordinates record where samples were taken, not where the populations are from. British, Indian Telugu and Sri Lankan Tamil samples share one Birmingham coordinate, and the Gujarati samples sit in Houston ([IGSR API](https://www.internationalgenome.org/api/beta/population/_search)). Ensembl's GRCh37 mirror returns only the 1000 Genomes and TOPMed frequency sets, with no gnomAD groups ([Ensembl GRCh37](https://grch37.rest.ensembl.org/variation/human/rs4988235?pops=1;content-type=application/json)). The Geography of Genetic Variants API returned server errors when tested ([GGV](https://popgen.uchicago.edu/ggv/)). Both findings argue for building every frequency table offline and depending on no third-party API while the app runs.

HGDP frequencies for each population exist only inside gnomAD's combined HGDP and 1000 Genomes VCF files, which reach 295 GB for chromosome 2. Those files carry per-population frequency fields and small index files, so pulling out a chip panel is a one-time offline job rather than a download ([gnomAD listing](https://storage.googleapis.com/storage/v1/b/gcp-public-data--gnomad/o?prefix=release/3.1.2/vcf/genomes/gnomad.genomes.v3.1.2.hgdp_tgp.chr2); [gnomAD header](https://storage.googleapis.com/gcp-public-data--gnomad/release/3.1.2/vcf/genomes/gnomad.genomes.v3.1.2.hgdp_tgp.chr21.vcf.bgz)). Storing 700,000 SNPs × 26 populations as 16-bit fixed-point values comes to about 36 MB raw, or roughly 10–15 MB compressed. That suits a same-origin file loaded on demand.

One number decides how much of the ancient-DNA plan is usable, and it has not been computed. It is the overlap between the 627,982 rsIDs on the Illumina GSA v3 manifest ([Illumina](https://support.illumina.com/downloads/infinium-global-screening-array-v3-0-product-files.html)) and AADR's Human Origins and 1240K panels. No ancient-ancestry feature should be promised until that overlap is measured.

## Published accuracy figures draw the line between evidence and theatre

Every analysis Lineage might offer has a published method with measured accuracy. Those numbers, not competitors' marketing, should decide what ships and how it is worded.

### Relationship odds need a genetic map and a simulator

ERSA is the easiest relationship model to implement exactly. It is a likelihood-ratio test over the number and lengths of shared segments. The number of segments is modelled as Poisson with mean a(rd + c)·p(t)/2^(d−1), where r ≈ 35.3 recombinations per generation and c = 22 autosomes. Segment lengths are exponential with mean 100/d cM ([Huff et al. 2011](https://jorde.genetics.utah.edu/pub/Huff_Witherspoon_GenomeRes_2011_ERSA.pdf)). **ERSA predicted the exact degree for 66% of 549 first- to fifth-degree pairs, and was within one degree for 97%.** Its power fell to 44% for ninth-degree relatives. Every quantity in the model is in centimorgans, so bundling a genetic map is a prerequisite, not an optimisation. Without a map, Lineage today reports lengths in megabases and uses a 7 Mb threshold as a stand-in for 7 cM. That is the 1 Mb ≈ 1 cM substitution the IBIS authors mark "NOT RECOMMENDED" ([IBIS README](https://github.com/williamslab/ibis/blob/master/README.md)).

Thresholds should come from the literature too. In 23andMe data containing 2,952 parent–child trios, **more than 67% of detected 2–4 cM segments were false positives** ([Durand et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104314/)). That is why AncestryDNA discards segments under 8 cM ([AncestryDNA white paper](https://www.ancestrycdn.com/support/us/2020/07/2020whitepaper.pdf)). IBIS works without phased data, in the same algorithm class as Lineage's error-tolerant scan. Its defaults are 7 cM, 436 markers and 0.004 errors per marker for half-identical segments, and 2 cM, 186 markers and 0.008 for fully identical ones ([IBIS README](https://github.com/williamslab/ibis/blob/master/README.md)). Adopting those defaults gives every Lineage threshold a citation. Fully identical regions are the cleanest discriminator available without phasing: full siblings share about 25% of the genome on both copies, and other relatives almost none ([Caballero et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6944377/)).

For probabilities, the honest routes are ERSA's likelihood or pedigree simulation. DNA Painter's widely quoted percentages do not come from the Shared cM Project. They were digitised from Figure 5.2 of AncestryDNA's simulation white paper ([DNA Painter](https://dnapainter.com/blog/introducing-the-updated-shared-cm-tool/)). The Shared cM Project v4 draws on 55,418 crowd submissions. Its minimums and maximums are the extremes left after trimming 0.5% from each tail, and its averages leave out pairs that share nothing ([Shared cM Project v4](https://thegeneticgenealogist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Shared-cM-Project-Version-4.pdf)). Lineage's current rule is to list every relationship whose observed range contains the total and to invent no probabilities. That is the correct use of the table, and it should stay as the "observed ranges" view.

Simulation adds what totals cannot. Ped-sim shows that maternal half-siblings share on average 51.9 segments, against 37.1 for paternal half-siblings. Modelling crossover interference reduces the spread of sharing by 10.4% on average, from siblings through second cousins. Sex-specific maps widen it by 4.2%, and the model with both features fits real data best ([Caballero et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6944377/)). Sharing on the X chromosome adds information about how many women sit on the path between two relatives ([Buffalo et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5012405/)).

The working tree already holds an uncommitted pedigree simulator with a sex-averaged map and no interference, and it says so in its output. By ped-sim's measurements, leaving out interference inflates the spread of sharing by roughly a tenth, and a sex-averaged map cannot reproduce the maternal–paternal difference in segment counts. The next step is Housworth–Stahl interference on Bhérer's sex-specific maps. The model should also score segment count, longest segment, fully identical sharing and the X together, not total cM alone.

### Ancestry percentages measure similarity to reference panels

Lineage's supervised EM against the five 1000 Genomes super-populations is ADMIXTURE's supervised mode. The method's authors restrict that mode to cases where "reference individuals can be assigned to ancestral populations with certainty and ancestral populations are fairly homogeneous" ([Alexander & Lange 2011](http://dalexander.github.io/preprints/admixture-enhancements-preprint.pdf)). The same paper explains the familiar artefact of small spurious percentages. Errors in reference frequencies inflate the estimated differences between source populations: in its simulation, estimated F_ST was 0.024 unsupervised and 0.019 supervised, against a true 0.010. As a result, "individuals will be ascribed a greater degree of admixture than they actually possess".

The 1000 Genomes populations are homogeneous only loosely, and they include diaspora samples: Utah residents stand in for north-west Europe and Gujaratis sampled in Houston for Gujarat ([IGSR API](https://www.internationalgenome.org/api/beta/population/_search)). The honest label is "the share of your genome best explained by each reference panel". It should come with intervals that can include zero. Beside it should sit a ranking of the nearest populations by log-likelihood, which needs nothing beyond the frequency table.

A "you are here" PCA plot is a different matter. Projecting one genome onto axes computed from other people shrinks it toward the centre of the plot. The corrections are smartpca's least-squares and shrinkage modes, or LASER's Procrustes approach. Both need the reference genotypes, not just allele frequencies ([EIGENSOFT README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DReichLab/EIG/master/POPGEN/README); [LASER](https://genome.sph.umich.edu/wiki/LASER)). A frequency table therefore cannot honestly place a person on a PCA.

Two sources set the wording for any such plot. Elhaik's 2022 critique holds that PCA outcomes "can be artifacts of the data" ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-14395-4)); no published rebuttal was retrieved for this report. In 2023 the National Academies asked researchers to say "genetic similarity" rather than "ancestry" ([NASEM](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26902/chapter/2)). If Lineage ships a PCA, it should compute the axes offline from open genotypes and publish the loadings and reference scores, so anyone can reproduce a projection. It should also caption the shrinkage. Publishing the space in this way is exactly what G25's private coordinate space prevents.

No published method supports chromosome painting finer than continents. FLARE reaches r² = 0.993 for three-way continental admixture with 10,000 reference individuals per panel. In a four-way test that includes Japanese and Chinese, FLARE falls to 0.888 and RFMix to 0.666 ([Browning et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9943733/)).

Users feel the same limit. Behnamian's 2026 study of 58,133 posts found disputes concentrated where reference panels overlap: 23.5% of posts about Turkish results disputed them, 19.7% for Greek and 18.5% for Scandinavian. Self-identified ethnicity matched the result in only 61.8% of cases. The authors recommend telling users about panel composition and about the difference between genetic ancestry and ethnic identity ([Behnamian 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13221540/)).

### Ancient models describe populations, not a person

The textbook three-source model of European ancestry is a finding about populations. Corded Ware people carried about 73.1 ± 2.2% Yamnaya-related ancestry ([Haak et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5048219/)). Early-farmer ancestry runs from about 30% in the Baltic to 90% in the Mediterranean, and Ancient North Eurasian ancestry reaches about 20% ([Lazaridis et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4170574/)). Those intervals come from pooled samples. qpAdm, the standard tool for these models, copes well with low coverage and with ancient samples that have one allele read per site (pseudo-haploid data). But it accepts any model the data fail to reject, so "multiple plausible models" can pass, and including too many reference populations can reject the true model ([Harney et al. 2021](https://academic.oup.com/genetics/article/217/4/iyaa045/6070149)). With a single person as the target, the standard errors are wide enough that almost any model survives. Matching shared segments between one person and an ancient genome is reliable only above 1× sequencing coverage ([Harney et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10502734/)).

A defensible ancient-ancestry product therefore has three parts. The first is a ranking of the closest reference groups, modern and ancient, computed from precomputed group frequencies and showing each group's sample count and SNP overlap. The second is the published population models, presented as history with their own uncertainty. The third is haplogroup context. If a per-person mixture is offered at all, it should show bootstrap intervals and every model that was not rejected, not a single pie chart. Global25 is not a published method, and Lineage should not use it.

### Runs of homozygosity need landmarks, not labels

Lineage already computes runs of homozygosity (ROH). The protocol in the standard review fits consumer files: a microarray of at least 300,000 SNPs, SNPs with allele frequency above 5%, and runs of at least 50 SNPs and 1.5 Mb ([Ceballos et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrg.2017.109)). The same review gives landmarks that need no probability model. People shown to be outbred carried runs of homozygosity up to about 4 Mb, but none longer. **A total length of runs above 160 Mb is a conservative boundary for parents who were first cousins or closer**, and children of first cousins have an expected inbreeding coefficient of 0.0625.

Siblings differ by about 10 Mb, however, and populations by hundreds. East Asians carry more ROH than Europeans, who carry more than South Asians, who carry more than sub-Saharan Africans. The result should therefore be a total plotted against the person's closest reference population, never a statement about their parents. No per-population reference distribution suitable for bundling was found. The genome length used as the denominator must be stated, or Lineage's figures will not be comparable with published ones.

### Eye colour is predictable from a chip file; a face is not

HIrisPlex-S is forensically validated. It uses multinomial logistic regressions over 41 SNPs to give probabilities for three eye colours, four hair colours and five skin tones ([Chaitanya et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29753263/)). Its accuracy is sharply uneven. For eye colour, the area under the curve (AUC, where 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect) is 0.95–0.96 for brown and 0.88–0.89 for blue, but only about 0.61 for intermediate. In a North German validation, **all 47 people with intermediate eye colour were misclassified as blue or brown.** Hair colour scores 0.71 for blond, 0.68 for brown and 0.83 for black, and the recommended 0.7 probability threshold discarded 74% of hair calls without improving accuracy ([North German evaluation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13116637/)). Skin tone reaches cross-validated AUCs of 0.72–0.74 for the three lightest categories, against 0.87–0.97 for the darkest two ([Walsh et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28500464/)). Accuracy also depends on who is being predicted. The eye model's 9,466 training individuals were 97.1% European ([Int J Legal Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00414-025-03709-6)), and in an admixed Brazilian sample the AUC for brown eyes fell to 0.67 ([FSI Genetics Supplement](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1875176819301465)).

Twenty-eight of the 41 SNPs are on the GSA v3 chip, including all six eye-colour markers, so eye colour is the one output a consumer file supports well ([Illumina](https://support.illumina.com/downloads/infinium-global-screening-array-v3-0-product-files.html)). The right display shows every category's probability with its published AUC beside it and lists which markers were present. It must never name a single "predicted colour". Lineage should ship this only after the reuse terms for the coefficients are settled.

No published method predicts a recognisable face from DNA. Lippert et al. 2017, the best-known claim, sequenced 1,061 people. Its own abstract concedes that for most traits "predictive accuracy beyond ancestry and demographic information is limited" ([Lippert et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5617305/)). Yaniv Erlich then matched its re-identification rate using only sex, age and self-reported ethnicity: 75% against the paper's 74% among ten candidates. He also found that nearly 80% of the explained variance in height came from sex markers ([Erlich, bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/185330v1.full-text); [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/news/geneticists-pan-paper-that-claims-to-predict-a-person-s-face-from-their-dna-1.22580)). The strongest face-related result, Sero et al. 2019, works in verification mode: it ranks given candidate faces against a DNA sample, with true-positive rates of 83% and 80%, and it does worse within a single ethnic group ([Sero et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-10617-y)).

Face-shape genome-wide association studies found 38 loci in 2,329 Europeans, 10 replicated loci in 10,115, and 32 regions in more than 6,000 Latin Americans ([Claes et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-018-0057-4); [Xiong et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.49898); [Bonfante et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7864580/)). None of the abstracts retrieved reports how much facial variation those loci explain. A 2023 forensic review says reaching the detail investigators want "requires further intensified scientific research" ([Kayser et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fsigen.2023.102870)). The one recent model reporting millimetre-scale error, Difface, was trained on 9,674 Han Chinese volunteers and has not been validated in other populations. Its faces also became more generic when less than 70% of its SNP data was available ([ZME Science](https://www.zmescience.com/science/news-science/ai-dna-3d-facial-reconstruction/)).

In forensic use, Parabon's Snapshot composites are finished by a forensic artist at a default body-mass index of 22. As reported by The Intercept, HIrisPlex-S co-developer Susan Walsh says "We can't even do a nose right now", and Mark Shriver calls the data "far too small to support the kind of individualizing predictions" ([The Intercept](https://theintercept.com/2025/02/02/forensic-dna-phenotyping-parabon-nanolabs-police/); [Parabon](https://snapshot.parabon-nanolabs.com/phenotyping)). The consumer "Face DNA" apps work the other way round, guessing ethnicity from a photograph ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.ineptlyawesome.facedna&hl=en_US)). Lineage should refuse face generation in plain words. In its place it can offer an explainer on the genetics of facial variation, together with the pigmentation probabilities.

## Intervals and evidence belong on the first screen

The commercial ancestry products share one skeleton: a ranked list of percentages, a map of soft-edged regions, and sometimes a chromosome painting. Each also has a single device for showing uncertainty. 23andMe has a confidence slider from 50% to 90% ([Data Mining DNA](https://dataminingdna.com/23andme-chromosome-painting/)), Ancestry shows ranges that can include zero ([Ancestry](https://www.ancestry.com/c/dna/ancestry-dna-ethnicity-estimate-update)), Living DNA offers Cautious, Standard and Complete views ([Who Are You Made Of](https://whoareyoumadeof.com/blog/how-to-understand-living-dna-results/)), and MyHeritage gives its genetic groups low, medium or high confidence ([MyHeritage](https://blog.myheritage.com/2020/12/myheritage-launches-genetic-groups/)).

The 2025 trend was to hide uncertainty: 23andMe's version 7 removed "Broadly" and "Unassigned" from the default view ([23andMe](https://www.23andme.org/blog/articles/ancestry-composition-version-7-is-here)). For a tool whose product is showing what is known, the opposite choice is the point of difference.

### Uncertainty sits in the primary view, not a detail pane

The primary ancestry view should be an estimate-and-interval list. Each reference group gets one row, nested under its super-population, with a dot for the estimate and a bar for the interval on a shared 0–100% axis. The figures are set in monospaced tabular digits, such as `EUR 62% (55–70)`. Rows whose interval includes zero are greyed out and labelled with Ancestry's plain phrase, "could be none".

A three-stop control using Living DNA's words (Cautious, Standard, Complete) is clearer than a raw percentage slider. At higher caution it folds sub-groups into their parent and grows an "Unassigned" row. Users of third-party raw-data services report difficulty understanding their results even when satisfied ([consumer study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5823680/)). So each group should carry a "why this might be wrong" card listing the samples that define it and how many there are, the groups it is often confused with, and a note that genetic similarity is not ethnicity. This answers Behnamian's recommendation directly.

When reference data or the method changes, "then and now" rows should show what moved. This follows 23andMe's Version History and MyHeritage's switch between versions 0.95 and 2.5. The same rows can compare two loaded kits, such as a parent and child. Each chart should be titled with its finding in one sentence, for example "You share 1,240 cM: most likely a first-cousin-level relative". Amanda Cox calls this annotation layer "the most important thing we do" ([data.europa.eu](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/visual-annotations-introduction)).

### Maps show where reference samples came from, not nations

The ancestry map should draw each reference population as a point at its sampling site, with a soft halo and no borders. MyHeritage leaves ethnicities borderless and outlines only its high-resolution groups. The halo's opacity shows the person's share, and the tooltip names the source, for example "YRI: Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria, 1000 Genomes phase 3".

For allele frequencies across populations, the reference design is the Geography of Genetic Variants browser. It draws one pie per population and rescales frequencies so that rare variants remain visible. It fades populations sampled at fewer than 30 people, and uses a force layout to keep points near their true origin without overlapping ([Marcus & Novembre 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5408806/)). On a 390-pixel phone screen the world map is about 358 pixels wide, and 26 pies shrink to about 12 pixels. There, the primary view should be a grouped dot strip: 26 rows under five super-population headers, each with a dot for the frequency and a 95% Wilson interval. The map then becomes a secondary locator showing where the samples came from.

Diaspora samples should get a dashed leader line from sampling site to ancestral region (Houston to Gujarat, for example). Charts should use gnomAD's term "genetic ancestry group" ([gnomAD](https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/news/2023-11-genetic-ancestry/)), with a source line under every chart in the style of Our World in Data ([OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/redesigning-our-interactive-data-visualizations)). A plain land outline, 20 KB compressed, avoids implying that genetic groups are nations.

### Chromosome views show evidence and paint only what is computed

Lineage should not draw an ancestry chromosome painting until a local-ancestry method produces calls segment by segment. Ancestry's own painter shows the risk: it painted whole chromosomes Scottish or Swedish where the user had no documented ancestry ([The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/chromosome-painting-at-ancestry/)), and painted output reads as literal truth. The evidence views should come first.

The segment browser for comparing two kits should have one row per chromosome, with width proportional to genetic length. Half-identical segments take a mid tone. Fully identical segments take a dark tone plus a diagonal hatch, so the distinction does not rely on colour alone. Segments below the threshold sit at 30% opacity, and a thin tier marks those above it, as GEDmatch does with its blue bar ([GEDmatch tutorial](https://nebula.org/blog/gedmatch-tutorial/)). Each row ends with its cM total and segment count. On a phone the 23 rows stack in about 460 pixels, and tapping a segment opens its positions, length and SNP count.

With several matches overlaid, each gets its own colour up to FamilyTreeDNA's cap of seven ([FTDNA](https://help.familytreedna.com/hc/en-us/articles/360004684575-Chromosome-Browser-Guide)); beyond that, colour by cluster. DNA Painter's highlight, a shadow around one match's segments with a full-height track behind them, singles out one match ([DNA Painter](https://dnapainter.com/blog/latest-dna-painter-updates-highlighting-a-match-more/)).

The chromosome base layer should be hand-drawn from 4.6–6.6 KB of cytoband data rather than taken from Ideogram.js, which is 115 KB compressed ([Ideogram](https://github.com/eweitz/ideogram)). Runs of homozygosity work as a two-tier track, following GEDmatch's "Are Your Parents Related?" view, with a caption saying that short runs are normal ([The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/revisiting-the-are-your-parents-related-tool-at-gedmatch/)). Trait SNPs can appear as ticks that open their evidence cards. A marker-density track can show where the chip actually looked.

### Clusters, relationship odds and fan charts for the genealogy tabs

**Relationship panel.** Pair ranked likelihood bars by relationship group with the Shared cM Project histogram for the selected group, and mark the user's total as a labelled line. DNA Painter hides its caveats on phones; Lineage should keep them as a collapsible "How sure is this?" row ([DNA Painter](https://dnapainter.com/blog/introducing-the-updated-shared-cm-tool/)).

**Cluster matrix.** Clustering follows Genetic Affairs' AutoCluster. Matches sit on both axes in cluster order, and grey cells mark matches that fall into two clusters, which endogamy multiplies ([DNAeXplained](https://dna-explained.com/2018/12/04/autoclustering-by-genetic-affairs/)). At 390 pixels a 50-match matrix leaves 7-pixel cells. Phones should get cluster cards instead, each listing members, cM range and shared surnames and places.

**Leeds colouring and hypotheses.** The Leeds method colours second- to third-cousin matches (about 90–400 cM) into four grandparent lines ([Dana Leeds](https://www.danaleeds.com/the-leeds-method/)). DNA Painter's WATO scores hypothesised positions in a tree ([WATO FAQ](https://dnapainter.com/help/wato-faq)). Together they turn match exports plus a GEDCOM into the features that Pro Tools and Tier 1 sell.

**Family trees.** The fan chart should be the headline tree view: 180° with four to five generations on phones, and five to seven rings on desktop. It should offer FamilySearch-style colour modes, such as birth country capped at nine colours ([FamilySearch](https://www.familysearch.org/en/blog/genealogy-fan-chart)). It should also offer an "expected DNA share" mode at 2^−k, with the outer rings hatched "may share no DNA". Nine generations back, your maternal autosomes arrive in about 286 chunks spread over 256 ancestors, so some ancestors contribute nothing ([Coop](https://gcbias.org/2013/11/04/how-much-of-your-genome-do-you-inherit-from-a-particular-ancestor/)). Pedigree and hourglass views should render four generations either side of a focus person and re-centre on click, the pattern used by Topola and webtrees ([webtrees fan chart](https://github.com/magicsunday/webtrees-fan-chart); [Topola](https://github.com/PeWu/topola)). Descendants read best as an indented list on phones.

### An ancient time map carries the narrative

Two ancient-DNA browsers, DORA and the AADR Visualizer, settle on one pattern: dated samples on a Natural Earth base, a histogram of samples in 100-year bins with a range selector, and a card for each sample linking its publication ([DORA](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/52/W1/W54/7671306); [AADR Visualizer](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformaticsadvances/article/5/1/vbaf199/8238351)). Human AGEs adds haplogroup and admixture layers ([Human AGEs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10320146/)).

Lineage's version should colour dots by age on a perceptual sequential colour ramp and cluster dense areas with counts. It should show only samples flagged "Pass" by default, with the other flags visible. It should highlight every sample on the user's own Y or mitochondrial branch. Tapping a dot opens a card with the date and dating method, place, data type, haplogroups and DOI. That card is the honest counterpart of MyHeritage's "notable individuals". On phones, an era stepper ("8000–6000 BP") replaces the two-thumb range selector, and autoplay is off when the user prefers reduced motion. A share card built as an image in the browser can carry the top groups with intervals, a mini map, one "we're not sure about" line and the method version. It must never carry genotypes.

### Colour, access and weight: an 85 KB stack

Colour-blind-safe categorical palettes stop at seven to nine hues. For the five super-populations, Okabe-Ito (without black) or Paul Tol's muted scheme kept every pair distinguishable under simulated colour-vision deficiency in the research for this report ([Paul Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/)). In the same simulation, d3's Tableau10 had six near-identical pairs for red-green deficiency. Green-Armytage's 26-colour alphabet, described as "near the maximum number reliably distinguishable" for normal vision ([Help:Distinguishable colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Distinguishable_colors)), had 20–22 confusable pairs.

Twenty-six populations therefore need a hue family per super-population, with lightness steps within each family. That follows 23andMe's geographic gradient. The three-letter code should appear as a direct label, and there should always be a table. Dark mode needs its own tints, because no palette clears 3:1 contrast on both white and near-black.

The accessibility bar should be Chartability's critical tests ([Chartability](https://chartability.github.io/POUR-CAF/)): a caption stating the takeaway, a "show as table" toggle, keyboard navigation between data points, and text no smaller than 12 pixels. Add long descriptions as the W3C recommends ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/)), touch targets of at least 24 pixels ([WCAG 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)), and no animation when the user has asked for reduced motion ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)). Every number should use tabular figures ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/fonts-for-data-visualization)). On phones, annotations should become a numbered key below the chart ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/better-more-responsive-annotations-in-datawrapper-data-visualizations)).

Individual d3 modules cover all of this for about 85 KB compressed, measured with esbuild ([npm: d3-geo](https://www.npmjs.com/package/d3-geo)):

| Module | Used for | Compressed size |
|---|---|---|
| d3-geo | Map projections | 9 KB |
| topojson-client with the 110m land outline | Reading and drawing the base map | 21 KB |
| d3 charting modules | Scales, shapes and axes | 19 KB |
| d3-zoom | Pan and zoom | 16 KB |
| d3-force | Keeping map points apart | 4 KB |
| d3-contour and d3-delaunay | PCA density shading and tap targets | 10 KB |
| d3-hierarchy | Fan and pedigree layouts | 2 KB |
| scrollama | Scroll-driven explainers | 2 KB |

MapLibre (281 KB), igv.js (432 KB) and Observable Plot (131 KB) are not worth their weight here. Topola (52 KB, Apache-2.0) is worth adding only if hourglass charts with multi-spouse families are needed. Map and tree code should load only when those views open. Lineage also appears in the portfolio's laptop and phone iframes, so its first screen should read at thumbnail size, with one bold graphic and large numerals.

The list of things not to build matters as much. Face generation, Global25 coordinates, single personal ancient breakdowns and ancient "matches" by shared segments from chip files are unsupported by the evidence. Disease, carrier and drug-response reports fall outside Lineage's own scope rule. Anything derived from SNPedia, the openSNP dump or FamilyTreeDNA's Mitotree is barred by its licence or by its creators' stated wishes. Scraping vendor sites, and any server that receives genotypes, would reintroduce the legal and custody risks that the rest of this plan removes.

## Conclusion

The competitive logic of consumer genomics has inverted since 2023. A large database used to be the moat. After the 23andMe breach and sale, the GEDmatch incidents and the FBI's search of MyHeritage, it is also a liability, and the companies that keep one are charging more for the analysis built on top of it. That analysis layer is where the small companies make their money, and its raw material is public: CC0 ancient genomes, reference panels free of embargo, openly licensed haplogroup trees and published likelihood models. A browser tool that holds nothing can take that value away from them without ever competing on matches. It has to meet two conditions. It must prove its privacy claim technically, not state it as policy. And it must treat its accuracy policy as the product itself, not as a disclaimer.

The less obvious implication is that honesty and fun are not in tension where the data are good. The feature users share most, a named ancient person, has a defensible open-data version: a Y or mitochondrial branch shared with a dated, published individual, with the branch's age and the paper's DOI. That is more checkable than any "genetic proximity" score. Where the data are weak, as with faces and personal ancient percentages, saying so plainly and showing why sets Lineage apart in a market whose sellers put caveats in the fine print. The main remaining risks are legal rather than scientific: the unlicensed genetic maps, the HIrisPlex-S coefficients, the Shared cM Project's terms, and whether browser-only analysis falls under the new state statutes. All four are cheaper to settle with authors and a lawyer before launch than after it.
